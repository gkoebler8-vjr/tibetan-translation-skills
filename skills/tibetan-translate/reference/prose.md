# Prose rendering rules (the draft in Pass 1 and the style pass in Pass 3, prose units)

The English standard (`english.md`) applies in full. Prose relaxes the metrical rules and adds these.

## 1. Absolute rules

1. **Completeness.** Every semantic component lands: clauses, negations, quantifiers, honorifics,
   vocatives, images. **Splitting a long Tibetan sentence into several English sentences is
   encouraged. Dropping its connective is forbidden.** A relation that becomes a full stop turns an
   argument into a list of assertions.
2. **No additions.** No explanatory glosses, no clarifying phrases, no intensifiers the Tibetan
   lacks. Explanation belongs in a note (where the mode allows one), never in the body.
3. **No apparatus** outside academic mode: no square brackets (supplied words are integrated
   silently), no parenthetical Wylie or Sanskrit, no "lit.", no folio numbers, no hedging the
   Tibetan does not have.
4. **Terminology** per the glossary; deviations flagged, never silent.
5. **Person.** "You" wherever the Tibetan instructs or addresses; "one" only in general statements;
   never mixed; never "he or she".
6. **Register** per `modes.md` §2, decided before drafting and held across the unit; a shift is
   marked where it happens.
7. **Verse is not prose.** A verse citation inside the unit is rendered by tibetan-verse and set as
   a verse block. Never paraphrase verse into prose except under a house rule for long padas.

## 2. Sentence discipline

- Median sentence **16–20 words**, ceiling about **35**. Never two 30-plus-word sentences in a row.
- Tibetan's chains of converbs (`nas … te … zhing … pas …`) become several English sentences, with
  the connective made explicit at the seam. This is the single highest-yield move in prose
  translation from Tibetan.
- One idea per sentence; one movement per paragraph. End-weight: the new information last.
- Strong finite verbs over nominalizations: "he relied on his teacher", not "his reliance upon".
- **Process language.** For devotion, blessing, ripening, realization: never mechanical causation
  ("causes", "produces", "automatically", "mechanism"). Use the text's own images and preparation
  language: "opens you to", "prepares the ground for", "ripens into". Keep a Tibetan metaphor
  physical; never abstract an image into a concept.
- **Commentary scaffolding.** `… zhes pa ni … ste` keeps its shape: the quoted words, "means", the
  explanation. The reader must always know which words are the root's.

## 3. Citation formulas

Only these, with the work's fixed English short title in italics (tibetan-citations):

- Block quotation from a text: `In the *Title* it is said:` — a connective the Tibetan has before the
  frame (`de yang`, `de ltar yang`, `de bas na`) precedes it: `Accordingly, in the *Title* it is said:`,
  `And in the *Title* it is said:`
- Block quotation from a person: `Name said:`
- Short inline quotation: `as the *Title* says, "…"` / `as Name said, "…"`
- A second quotation following directly: `And it is said:`

Never a Sanskrit or Wylie title in the body (outside academic mode). Locators never in the body:
they go to the sources register.

## 4. The loop

1. Your reading of the unit, recorded as the construal sketch (SKILL.md §3; the full object of
   analysis.md only for a contested unit).
2. Draft from that reading, not from the Tibetan word order.
3. Split and connect: break the long periods; make every connective explicit at the seam.
4. Gates (english.md §1).
5. Carry consistency forward: a phrasing chosen for a recurring Tibetan expression binds for the
   rest of the piece; when a later unit forces revising an earlier choice, say so.
